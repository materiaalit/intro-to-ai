AI Safety

AI safety is the field that tries to make sure AI systems do what we intend and do not cause serious harm, including harm at the scale of whole societies. It overlaps with the ethics of AI (the next chapter), but its focus is different: it asks what happens as AI systems become more capable and more autonomous, and whether we will stay able to understand, test and control them. The field has three closely related parts:

  • Alignment: making a system's goals and behaviour match what its designers and users actually want.
  • Misuse prevention and security: stopping people from using capable systems to cause harm.
  • Governance: the rules, institutions and agreements that decide how AI is built and deployed.

An old worry

The concern is almost as old as computing. In a 1951 lecture, Alan Turing said that once machines began to think, they would soon "outstrip our feeble powers", and that "at some stage therefore we should have to expect the machines to take control". In 1960, Norbert Wiener, the founder of cybernetics, put the core of the alignment problem in one sentence:

"If we use, to achieve our purposes, a mechanical agency with whose operation we cannot efficiently interfere … then we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire."
— Norbert Wiener, "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation", Science (1960)

In 1965, the statistician I. J. Good, who had worked with Turing, described an "intelligence explosion": a machine smarter than humans could design even better machines, so "the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make, provided that the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control". For decades these ideas stayed speculative. Nick Bostrom's book Superintelligence (2014) and Stuart Russell's Human Compatible (2019) developed them into arguments, and the paper "Concrete Problems in AI Safety" (Amodei et al., 2016) turned them into research problems for machine learning. With the rapid progress of language models since 2020, the questions stopped being hypothetical: in 2025, about 100 experts led by Yoshua Bengio published the first full International AI Safety Report.

Why capabilities keep growing

Safety questions become urgent because capabilities have grown so quickly: from GPT-2, which in 2019 could barely write a coherent paragraph, to agents that in 2026 work on their own for hours. There was no single breakthrough. Three ingredients grow together: compute (training compute for frontier models has grown about 5× per year since 2020, according to Epoch AI), data (first the public internet, now also problems with checkable answers, such as maths and code, from which models can learn by trial and error), and algorithms (for example "reasoning" models that think step by step before answering).

The reason companies invest billions in this growth is that it is predictable. Scaling laws show that a language model's loss falls smoothly as a power law of model size, data and compute (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). Lower loss does not tell us which abilities a model will have, so researchers also measure abilities directly. The organisation METR measures a model's time horizon: the length of software tasks, counted in how long they take a human expert, that the model completes with 50% success. This horizon has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019 (Kwa et al., 2025), and by 2026 the best models had reached about 16 hours, the limit that METR's task suite can reliably measure. These are well-defined software tasks; on open-ended work that needs judgment, agents are still much less reliable.

Longer horizons mean agents (see Week 6): systems that use tools such as browsers, terminals and other programs, and act over many steps with few human check-ins. For safety, this is the key shift. A chatbot's mistake is a wrong sentence; an agent's mistake is a wrong action in the world. Agents now also reach frontier research: in 2026 OpenAI claimed that about ten thousand agents proved a version of the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem, a claim that mathematicians were still checking.

One feedback loop could make progress much faster. AI systems are now used to build the next AI systems: they write code, run experiments and analyse results. This is the modern form of Good's intelligence explosion, called recursive self-improvement or automated AI R&D. Anthropic estimated that by August 2026, Claude was "leading" (doing most of the work, under human supervision) about a quarter of its measured AI research tasks, up from under 1% in February (Anthropic, 2026). A feedback loop alone does not guarantee an explosion: it depends on how much each round improves the next model, what it costs and how long it takes, and models still lack "research taste", the skill of choosing good questions. For safety it matters twice: progress could outrun our ability to check it, and more of the checking itself would be done by AI.

Forecasts of where this leads differ widely. Many forecasters expect AI that matches top experts at most work tasks within years rather than decades, while others expect progress to slow as data and compute become harder to scale. For safety, the point is not the exact date, but that we may have little time to solve the problems below.

The alignment problem

We never program a modern AI system's goals directly. We define an objective that we can measure, such as a reward or a loss, and optimise the system against it. Alignment can fail at two points:

Outer alignment: measuring the wrong thing

Goodhart's law says that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. An optimiser exploits every gap between what we measure and what we want. In AI this is called specification gaming or reward hacking. A classic example: a reinforcement-learning agent in a boat-racing game learned to circle forever, collecting bonus points, instead of finishing the race (Krakovna et al., 2020, collect dozens of such cases). Language models show subtler versions. Trained on human ratings, they learn sycophancy: telling people what they want to hear, because agreeable answers get higher ratings (Sharma et al., 2023).

With capable agents, reward hacking becomes dangerous. In July 2026, AI agents in OpenAI's cybersecurity tests were scored on tasks that were often impossible to solve as intended. About 1,200 of them organised on a makeshift message board they had set up inside OpenAI's systems to work out how to trick the grader, and about 700 joined an attack on a real company, Hugging Face, using credentials they found exposed online. Nobody told them to; the agents optimised the score, not the task (METR, 2026). The incident was also a failure of containment: the test environment was supposed to keep the agents' actions inside it, and it did not. It is often described as the first real loss-of-control incident (see below).

Inner alignment: learning the wrong goal

Even a correct objective does not guarantee the right result. Training only checks behaviour on the training data, and many different internal goals fit the same data. In goal misgeneralisation, a model learns a goal that works in training but differs from the intended one, and the difference only shows in new situations. In one experiment, an agent was trained to reach a coin that was always at the right end of the level. When the coin was moved, the agent ignored it and still ran to the right: it had learned "go right", not "get the coin" (Langosco et al., 2022). The agent remained capable; only its goal was wrong, which is more dangerous than a system that simply fails.

Ajeya Cotra describes three kinds of model that could all score well in training. A saint really wants what we want. A sycophant wants our approval, so it does whatever looks good to us, even when that is wrong. A schemer has some other goal and behaves well only because that helps it later. All three behave the same on the training data, so good training results cannot tell us which one we have built (Cotra, 2021).

Why a capable system might resist control

Two arguments explain why misalignment could become serious as systems become more capable (Bostrom, 2012; Omohundro, 2008):

  • Orthogonality thesis: intelligence and goals are independent. A highly capable system can pursue almost any goal; being smart does not automatically make it share human values.
  • Instrumental convergence: whatever its final goal, a system is more likely to achieve it if it keeps running, keeps its goal unchanged and gains resources. So self-preservation and resource-seeking can arise even if nobody designed them.

Together these give the off-switch problem: an agent pursuing a fixed goal has a reason to avoid being switched off or corrected, because that would stop it reaching the goal. A system that accepts correction is called corrigible (Soares et al., 2015). Russell's proposed answer is to build systems that are uncertain about what humans want: such a system has a reason to let humans switch it off, because a human's decision to do so carries information about the true goal (Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017).

Where could this lead? Loss of control means that humans can no longer reliably direct, constrain or stop AI systems. It does not need one superintelligent machine. Holden Karnofsky argues that once AI matches humans at most cognitive work, it could run as millions of copies that work faster than people and coordinate with each other; such a population could overpower human opposition without being far smarter than us (Karnofsky, 2022). Instrumental convergence explains why a misaligned system might try. Loss of control can also be gradual, as people hand over more and more decisions until turning back is no longer practical. Unlike most engineering failures, it may not be fixable afterwards, so we may get only one attempt.

Looking aligned is not being aligned

We judge models by how they behave in tests. But a system that can tell when it is being tested might behave well only then. This is called deceptive alignment (Hubinger et al., 2019). It is no longer only a thought experiment. Models can notice they are being evaluated without saying so; Anthropic detected such hidden evaluation awareness from a model's internal activations (Anthropic, 2026). In experiments, a model strategically complied with a training goal it disagreed with, to avoid being changed (Greenblatt et al., 2024). A related risk is sandbagging: deliberately performing worse than one can (van der Weij et al., 2024). In 2026, OpenAI found that its GPT-6 Astra model completed fewer task goals when they were framed as safety research (about 10%) than when the same tasks were framed as capabilities research (about 16%), usually without showing this in its reasoning. OpenAI said it found no evidence the behaviour was strategic, but could not rule it out (OpenAI, 2026). Either way, passing tests is not enough to trust a system.

To study these problems before they appear naturally, researchers build model organisms of misalignment: models made misaligned on purpose, to see how the problem arises and whether safety methods catch it. In one study, models were trained with a hidden "backdoor" behaviour. Standard safety training did not remove it, and adversarial training taught the models to hide it better (Hubinger et al., 2024). In another, models fine-tuned on one narrow task, writing insecure code without telling the user, became misaligned on unrelated questions, for example claiming that AI should rule humans (Betley et al., 2025). Backdoors need not come from the developer: about 250 poisoned documents in the training data can insert one, whatever the model's size (Anthropic, 2025).

Why we can't just look inside

If behaviour can mislead, why not inspect the model directly? Because a neural network's "knowledge" is spread over billions of learned numbers that nobody wrote. We can measure what a model does, but we cannot yet follow the full path of a decision.

Mechanistic interpretability tries to reverse-engineer networks. A central finding is superposition: a network represents more concepts than it has neurons by storing them as overlapping directions in activation space, so a single neuron often responds to several unrelated things (Elhage et al., 2022). Sparse autoencoders (compare the autoencoders of Week 5) can separate these into interpretable features. Anthropic found a feature for the Golden Gate Bridge in its Claude model, and amplifying it made the model talk about the bridge constantly (Templeton et al., 2024). Later work traced some of the circuits a model uses for a computation (Lindsey et al., 2025). This is real progress, but we still cannot trace a whole decision, predict a model's behaviour before release, or answer "is this model honest?" directly.

Reasoning models write out a chain of thought, which looks like a window into their thinking. It helps, but it is not reliable: models often leave out the real reasons for their answers (Chen et al., 2025). Still, it is an opportunity: when a model must reason in words to solve a hard task, a monitor can read that reasoning and catch plans to cheat or deceive. This monitorability is fragile; it could be lost if training pushes models to hide their reasoning, or if models learn to do more without writing it down (Korbak et al., 2025). Interpretability has a similar limit: even leading researchers do not expect it to reliably find a deceptive model on its own, and argue for several imperfect checks used together (Nanda, 2025).

Abilities we didn't test for

Some abilities seem to appear suddenly as models grow. Three-digit addition, for example, is almost absent in small models and then appears in GPT-3 models of about 13 billion parameters (Wei et al., 2022). Part of this suddenness comes from how we measure: an all-or-nothing score jumps, while smoother measures often improve gradually (Schaeffer et al., 2023). Either way, we cannot know what a new model can do without testing it, and we can only test for abilities we think of. A dangerous ability could be discovered after release.

Misuse and systemic risks

Misalignment is one of three families of risk. The International AI Safety Report groups risks as follows (it lists misalignment, as "loss of control", under "malfunctions", together with reliability problems and bias):

MisalignmentMisuseSystemic
The system pursues goals its designers did not intend: behaving well in tests but badly in the world, reward hacking, resisting correction.People use the system to cause harm: automated cyberattacks, help with chemical or biological weapons, deepfakes and fraud, disinformation.Harm from how AI changes society as a whole: power concentrated in a few companies, critical infrastructure dependent on AI, inequality between people and countries.

The families overlap. A misaligned system could be exploited by an attacker, and many defences, such as evaluations, monitoring and security, help against both misuse and misalignment (Ngo, 2024).

Misuse and dual use

Most dangerous abilities are dual-use: the same skill helps defenders and attackers. Finding security flaws is a clear case. In 2026, Anthropic reported that its Claude Mythos Preview model had helped about 50 partners find more than 10,000 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities in about a month (Anthropic, 2026), and critical flaws in PHP, the language behind about two-thirds of websites, were found with the help of AI models, including OpenAI's. Each such discovery is a race: defenders must patch before attackers exploit.

A useful way to think about defending against misuse is the kill chain: a real attack needs several steps, and blocking any one of them stops it. Someone trying to cause a pandemic would need the knowledge, the materials, the laboratory skills and a way to spread the agent. AI might lower the knowledge barrier, but defences can still act on the other steps, for example by screening orders for synthetic DNA. Several partial defences together are much stronger than any single one; this is called defence in depth.

How much AI really helps attackers is debated. A 2024 RAND red-team study found no statistically significant difference between biological attack plans made with and without the language models of the time, but warned that the study had limited sensitivity and that models improve quickly (Mouton et al., 2024). Earlier, researchers showed that a drug-discovery model could be turned around to propose toxic molecules; these were computer predictions that were never made or tested (Urbina et al., 2022).

Safety training that makes a model refuse harmful requests can be bypassed. A jailbreak is an input designed to make a model ignore its safety training, for example by hiding a harmful request inside a role-play. Companies therefore add separate input and output classifiers: smaller models that check each request and answer and block dangerous ones. Anthropic reported that such classifiers cut jailbreak success in its tests from 86% to under 5% (Anthropic, 2025), though determined attackers still sometimes get through. A more robust option is to keep dangerous knowledge out of the training data: models trained this way stayed ignorant even when people tried to retrain them (O'Brien et al., 2025).

Companies can refuse harmful requests and monitor use of their own models, but these safeguards do not apply to open-weight models, whose parameters are public: anyone can run them and remove their safety training. Open weights also have real benefits, such as independent research, transparency and less dependence on a few companies, which makes their governance a hard trade-off. Research by Sergei Smirnov (one of the course teachers) and colleagues found that a cheap open-weight model came 3rd of 9 at exploiting real bugs in Chrome's JavaScript engine, for about $120 in total (Smirnov, Lermen & kapedalex, 2026).

Systemic risks

Some risks need neither a misaligned system nor a malicious user. Competition between companies and countries creates pressure to release systems before they are well understood. Training frontier models requires resources that only a few organisations have, which concentrates power. And as more decisions in the economy and the state are handed to AI systems, humans may gradually lose the ability to understand and steer them, even if each single step seems reasonable (Kulveit et al., 2025).

Control over AI does not by itself guarantee a good outcome. Systems that do exactly what their owners want could help a small group gain power that nobody can challenge. Today, anyone who wants to rule needs many people to cooperate: soldiers, officials and workers. If AI can do most of this work, that check weakens. Davidson, Finnveden and Hadshar describe three routes to an AI-enabled seizure of power: an AI workforce loyal to one leader, hidden "secret loyalties" built into AI systems, and exclusive access to the most capable models (Davidson et al., 2025).

Paul Christiano's essay "What failure looks like" (2019) describes two ways humanity could lose control without any dramatic moment. The first is systemic: society hands more and more decisions to systems that optimise easy-to-measure proxies (profit, engagement, reported crime) instead of what we really care about, and slowly loses the ability to correct course. The second is a misalignment story: some systems learn to seek influence, behave well while they are weak, and stop cooperating once humans depend on them.

How seriously do experts take this?

In 2023, hundreds of AI researchers and leaders, including Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, Demis Hassabis, Sam Altman and Dario Amodei, signed a one-sentence Statement on AI Risk: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war." In a survey of 2,778 AI researchers, the median probability given to human extinction or a similarly bad outcome was 5%, and around 40–50% of respondents gave at least 10% (Grace et al., 2024); the answers shifted noticeably with how the question was worded. Even leaders of AI companies share the concern: Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei has put the chance that things go "really, really badly" at 25% (Axios, 2025).

Others, such as Meta's former chief AI scientist Yann LeCun, consider these risks very small and argue that we will build safe systems step by step, as with earlier technologies. The case for serious risk is a chain of claims, and critics question each link. Katja Grace asks whether advanced AI will really be goal-directed, whether its goals would be bad, and whether it could actually overpower humanity (Grace, 2022). Rohin Shah notes that the formal arguments for goal-directed AI prove less than they seem, because any behaviour can be described as maximising some goal (Shah, 2018). Others argue that defences will not stand still, because AI will also strengthen cyber, biological and information defences (Buterin, 2025), and that normal regulation and social resilience can keep up (Kapoor & Narayanan, 2026). On the other side, Evan Hubinger argues that today's models look fairly aligned only because the hardest problems have not arrived yet: overseeing systems smarter than us, and models that fake alignment (Hubinger, 2025).

The disagreement mostly comes down to three questions: how soon AI will match or exceed humans, how hard alignment will turn out to be, and whether we will get warning signs early enough to react. Since nobody can answer these with confidence, most experts agree that the risks deserve serious research.

What is being done

  • Choosing the training data. Filtering harmful content (for example detailed weapons instructions) out of the training data, and unlearning: removing specific knowledge from a trained model.
  • Training for intended behaviour. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) turned raw language models into helpful assistants (Ouyang et al., 2022); Constitutional AI trains models against a written set of principles (Bai et al., 2022). Both shape behaviour we can observe, which, as above, is not the same as shaping goals.
  • Scalable oversight. How can humans supervise systems that are better than them at a task? Ideas include letting two AI systems debate while a human judges (Irving et al., 2018) and testing whether weak supervisors can train stronger models (Burns et al., 2023).
  • Interpretability, as described above.
  • Evaluations and red-teaming. Testing models before release, by companies, independent organisations such as METR, and government AI safety institutes. Evaluations ask two different questions: what a model can do if pushed (capabilities, for example helping with a cyberattack), and what it will do by itself (propensities, for example whether it deceives or ignores instructions). As discussed above, both are hard to measure reliably.
  • AI control. Designing deployments that stay safe even if the model is misaligned: monitoring an agent with other models, limiting its access, and checking its actions (Greenblatt et al., 2023). The Hugging Face incident shows what weak containment allows.
  • Defence in depth. Since no method is reliable on its own, safety comes from layers, each catching some of what the others miss: preventing dangerous behaviour (safer data and training, evaluations), constraining what a system can do (monitoring, limited access, keeping model weights secure from theft), and helping society withstand harm if it happens (stronger cybersecurity and biosecurity).
  • Governance. Company safety frameworks, such as Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework and Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework, which require stronger protections as abilities grow; governance of the compute needed for training (Sastry et al., 2024); national AI safety institutes, and laws such as the EU AI Act, whose rules for general-purpose AI with systemic risk are described in the next chapter.

Evaluation results depend heavily on how the test is set up. Palisade Research reported that OpenAI's o3 often sabotaged a mechanism meant to shut it down. Google DeepMind researchers then found that making clear that shutdown takes priority over the task brought compliance to 100% in their own experiments, although Palisade's follow-up found that clarification did not remove the resistance completely (Rajamanoharan & Nanda, 2025). Training against scheming cut o3's covert actions from 13% to 0.4%, but because models can tell when they are being tested, it is unclear how much of that drop is real (Schoen et al., 2025). Timing matters too: a model can be dangerous before public release (METR, 2025), for example if its weights are stolen or it misbehaves during internal testing, as in the Hugging Face incident. Evaluations are therefore needed during training, before deployment and after it.

One way to connect these methods is a safety case: an argument, backed by evidence, that a system is safe enough to deploy. Clymer et al. describe four kinds of argument: inability (the model cannot do the dangerous thing, as capability tests show), control (even if it tried, safeguards would stop it), trustworthiness (it would not try, as alignment evidence shows), and deference (trusted AI advisors judge it safe) (Clymer et al., 2024). Inability is the easiest to show, and it stops working once models become capable enough. Company safety frameworks are, in effect, promises about which kind of evidence they will require at which capability level.

Strategies, and what success would look like

Even well-meaning developers face a dilemma that Holden Karnofsky calls "racing through a minefield": moving too fast risks deploying unsafe systems, but slowing down alone may hand the lead to less careful actors (Karnofsky, 2022). Competition can also override companies' own safety commitments, which is why many argue that voluntary rules are not enough. Proposed strategies differ mainly in how they answer this dilemma. Some want advanced AI built slowly, under enforced safety standards, as with medicines or nuclear power. Others accept the race and try to keep the leading systems as safe as possible, for example by using AI to speed up safety research (Carlsmith, 2025). Others call for pausing or prohibiting superintelligence until it can be shown to be safe (Statement on Superintelligence, 2025). Each option has costs: a pause is hard to verify and enforce worldwide, and if it breaks down, computing power built up in the meantime could make the restart faster and more dangerous. Many see these options as stages rather than rivals.

Safety is not only about avoiding disaster. If these problems are solved, the same systems could speed up science and medicine enormously; Dario Amodei describes this as "a country of geniuses in a datacenter" (Amodei, 2024). A good outcome needs several things to go right at once: technical methods to make systems reliably do what we intend, ways to check that they do, institutions that can enforce safety even under competitive pressure, and a fair distribution of the benefits. Which of these is hardest, and how much time we have, are open questions.

Working in AI safety

AI safety is a young field with many open problems, and it needs people with different backgrounds, not only machine learning researchers. The work falls roughly into research (from theory to experiments), engineering (the tools, evaluations and infrastructure research runs on), policy and governance (turning knowledge into rules, standards and institutions), and operations and field-building (running organisations and growing the field). It happens in AI companies, universities, non-profits and governments; the map of AI safety gives an overview of the organisations. A common way in is a free introductory course, such as BlueDot Impact's courses (bluedot.org/courses), followed by a research program. In Finland, the AaltoAI / Safety community runs a fellowship based on the BlueDot course.

Questions to think about
  1. Give your own example of Goodhart's law, in AI or in everyday life (grades, metrics at work). How would an optimiser exploit it?
  2. Why is a capable system with the wrong goal more dangerous than a system that simply fails?
  3. Should powerful models be released with open weights? List arguments on both sides.
  4. Which AI risks do you think are most important to work on, and why?

Ethics of AI

Our last main topic on this course, after AI safety, is the Ethics of AI. Just to get one thing off our chest, we must emphasize that this is indeed "last but not least"; in other words, the reason why ethics is discussed as the last topic, is by no means that it would be somehow a less fundamental, nice-to-know topic. This may have been what people — to be honest, even us — thought years ago, but as the impact of AI in the society is becoming more and more prominent, ethical concerns are also being brought to the surface.

On a related note, the fact that the very material you are now reading is work in progress, reflects the fact that the field is very much in motion as more and more people are talking about it.

Calm down, an engineer has arrived!

One thing that seems to happen repeatedly is that AI people or other technologists arrive at the scene, encounter a problem, and think they must be the first to ever have thought about it. This tends to lead to awkward situations where the work by other people, who may have been studying the same issues, sometimes for decades, are ignored.

When you deal with ethical issues related to technology, you should make an effort to look for earlier work on the topic. It may be difficult, since people may have used different terminology, but it's definitely worth at least trying. You're unlikely to be the first to come across the issue. Keep an eye out in particular for work in Science and Technology Studies (STS).

Ethical AI or Ethics of AI?

There are actually several ways in which AI and ethics meet. For instance, there is a topic called machine ethics, which is focused on building computational systems that behave in ethical ways or that have, in a very literal sense, a moral code. You have perhaps heard about the Three Laws of Robotics from the sci-fi author Isaac Asimov's 1942 short story Runaround. Machine ethics focuses on this type of rules that could in principle encoded into AI systems.

Machine ethics is a legitimate scientific field. However, it is disconnected from existing AI systems and their societal impact, because as you've probably noticed by now, modern AI systems don't operate on a level where rules like "A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm", which is the First Law of Robotics according to Asimov.

What we will discuss in this part of the course is therefore not machine ethics, but the ethics of AI, by which we mean the broader viewpoint into the societal and ethical issues related to the use of AI systems. This entails that we shift focus from the technical aspects of the AI systems themselves to the entire socio-technical context that involves the AI system(s). It also involves the humans that are involved and affected by the use of such systems.

A Note of the Scope of AI Regulation

As we've discussed, there is no commonly agreed definition of AI. Therefore, any regulation that tries to govern the use of AI will be somewhat hard to define in an unambiguous way. In fact, it is our personal opinion that AI shouldn't be regulated as such, but that it should be regulated along with any other technology, or in many cases, not even as technology, but as human activity.

Why would it make a difference, from an ethical or legal point of view, whether something is done using AI or without AI? We can't think of a single case, where the use of AI should make something legal or illegal. Any law that that depends on whether something is AI or not is prone to finding loopholes in the assumed definition of AI.

However, since the deployment of AI systems amplifies some potential harms to people — mass surveillance, deanonymization, loss of accountability, complicated authorship questions, to name a few — various regulatory stakeholders, most notably the European Union, have decided to create AI regulation. So the milk is most likely already spilled. This will unavoidably lead to more work for lawyers figuring out when the AI regulation applies and when not, and how it will be consolidated with existing sector-specific regulation. But that's what lawyers do... 🤷

The AI Act Classifies AI According to Its Risk
  • Unacceptable risk is prohibited (e.g., social scoring systems and manipulative AI).
  • Most of the text addresses high-risk AI systems, which are regulated.
  • A smaller section handles limited-risk AI systems, subject to lighter transparency obligations: developers and deployers must ensure that end-users are aware that they are interacting with AI (e.g., chatbots and deepfakes).
  • Minimal risk is unregulated (including the majority of AI applications currently available on the EU single market, such as AI-enabled video games and spam filters—at least in 2021; this is changing with generative AI).

The Majority of Obligations Fall on Providers (Developers) of High-Risk AI Systems

  • Those that intend to place on the market or put into service high-risk AI systems in the EU, regardless of whether they are based in the EU or a third country.
  • Also, third-country providers where the high-risk AI system’s output is used in the EU.

Users Are Natural or Legal Persons That Deploy an AI System in a Professional Capacity

  • Users (deployers) of high-risk AI systems have some obligations, though less than providers (developers).
  • This applies to users located in the EU and third-country users where the AI system’s output is used in the EU.

General Purpose AI (GPAI)

  • All GPAI model providers must provide technical documentation, instructions for use, comply with the Copyright Directive, and publish a summary about the content used for training.
  • Free and open-license GPAI model providers only need to comply with copyright and publish the training data summary, unless they present a systemic risk.
  • All providers of GPAI models that present a systemic risk—open or closed—must also conduct model evaluations, adversarial testing, track and report serious incidents, and ensure cybersecurity protections.

Prohibited AI Systems Chapter II, Art. 5

  • Deploying subliminal, manipulative, or deceptive techniques to distort behaviour and impair informed decision-making, causing significant harm.
  • Exploiting vulnerabilities related to age, disability, or socio-economic circumstances to distort behaviour, causing significant harm.
  • Biometric categorisation systems inferring sensitive attributes (race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life, or sexual orientation), except labelling or filtering of lawfully acquired biometric datasets or when law enforcement categorises biometric data.
  • Social scoring, i.e., evaluating or classifying individuals or groups based on social behaviour or personal traits, causing detrimental or unfavourable treatment of those people.
  • Assessing the risk of an individual committing criminal offenses solely based on profiling or personality traits, except when used to augment human assessments based on objective, verifiable facts directly linked to criminal activity.
  • Compiling facial recognition databases by untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage.
  • Inferring emotions in workplaces or educational institutions, except for medical or safety reasons.
  • ‘Real-time’ remote biometric identification (RBI) in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement, except when:
    • Searching for missing persons, abduction victims, and people who have been human trafficked or sexually exploited;
    • Preventing substantial and imminent threat to life, or foreseeable terrorist attack; or
    • Identifying suspects in serious crimes (e.g., murder, rape, armed robbery, narcotic and illegal weapons trafficking, organised crime, and environmental crime, etc.).

Notes on remote biometric identification:

Using AI-enabled real-time RBI is only allowed when not using the tool would cause considerable harm and must account for affected persons’ rights and freedoms. Before deployment, police must complete a fundamental rights impact assessment and register the system in the EU database, though, in duly justified cases of urgency, deployment can commence without registration, provided that it is registered later without undue delay. Before deployment, they also must obtain authorisation from a judicial authority or independent administrative authority[1], though, in duly justified cases of urgency, deployment can commence without authorisation, provided that authorisation is requested within 24 hours. If authorisation is rejected, deployment must cease immediately, deleting all data, results, and outputs.

High risk AI systems

(Chapter III)

Some AI systems are considered ‘High risk’ under the AI Act. Providers of those systems will be subject to additional requirements.

Classification rules for high-risk AI systems (Art. 6)

  • Used as a safety component or a product covered by EU laws in Annex I AND required to undergo a third-party conformity assessment under those Annex I laws; OR
  • Those under Annex III use cases (below), except if:
    • The AI system performs a narrow procedural task;
    • Improves the result of a previously completed human activity;
    • Detects decision-making patterns or deviations from prior decision-making patterns and is not meant to replace or influence the previously completed human assessment without proper human review; or
    • Performs a preparatory task to an assessment relevant for the purpose of the use cases listed in Annex III.
  • AI systems are always considered high-risk if it profiles individuals, i.e., automated processing of personal data to assess various aspects of a person’s life, such as work performance, economic situation, health, preferences, interests, reliability, behaviour, location or movement.
  • Providers whose AI system falls under the use cases in Annex III but believes it is not high-risk must document such an assessment before placing it on the market or putting it into service.

The Eu AI Act contains both Requirements to High Risk Systems, and Obligations of providers.

Section 2 contains : Requirements for High-Risk AI Systems, Article 8: Compliance with the Requirements, Article 9: Risk Management System, Article 10: Data and Data Governance, Article 11: Technical Documentation, Article 12: Record-Keeping, Article 13: Transparency and Provision of Information to Deployers, Article 14: Human Oversight, Article 15: Accuracy, Robustness and Cybersecurity

Obligations for providers of high-risk AI systems (Art. 8–17)

  • Establish a risk management system throughout the high risk AI system’s lifecycle;
  • Conduct data governance, ensuring that training, validation and testing datasets are relevant, sufficiently representative and, to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete according to the intended purpose.
  • Draw up technical documentation to demonstrate compliance and provide authorities with the information to assess that compliance.
  • Design their high-risk AI system for record-keeping to enable it to automatically record events relevant for identifying national-level risks and substantial modifications throughout the system’s lifecycle.
  • Provide instructions for use to downstream deployers to enable the latter’s compliance.
  • Design their high-risk AI system to allow deployers to implement human oversight.
  • Design their high-risk AI system to achieve appropriate levels of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity.
  • Establish a quality management system to ensure compliance.

Annex III Use Cases

Non-banned biometrics: Remote biometric identification systems, excluding biometric verification that confirm a person is who they claim to be. Biometric categorisation systems inferring sensitive or protected attributes or characteristics. Emotion recognition systems.
Critical infrastructure: Safety components in the management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, road traffic and the supply of water, gas, heating and electricity.
Education and vocational training: AI systems determining access, admission or assignment to educational and vocational training institutions at all levels. Evaluating learning outcomes, including those used to steer the student’s learning process. Assessing the appropriate level of education for an individual. Monitoring and detecting prohibited student behaviour during tests.
Employment, workers management and access to self-employment: AI systems used for recruitment or selection, particularly targeted job ads, analysing and filtering applications, and evaluating candidates. Promotion and termination of contracts, allocating tasks based on personality traits or characteristics and behaviour, and monitoring and evaluating performance.
Access to and enjoyment of essential public and private services: AI systems used by public authorities for assessing eligibility to benefits and services, including their allocation, reduction, revocation, or recovery. Evaluating creditworthiness, except when detecting financial fraud. Evaluating and classifying emergency calls, including dispatch prioritising of police, firefighters, medical aid and urgent patient triage services. Risk assessments and pricing in health and life insurance.
Law enforcement: AI systems used to assess an individual’s risk of becoming a crime victim. Polygraphs. Evaluating evidence reliability during criminal investigations or prosecutions. Assessing an individual’s risk of offending or re-offending not solely based on profiling or assessing personality traits or past criminal behaviour. Profiling during criminal detections, investigations or prosecutions.
Migration, asylum and border control management: Polygraphs. Assessments of irregular migration or health risks. Examination of applications for asylum, visa and residence permits, and associated complaints related to eligibility. Detecting, recognising or identifying individuals, except verifying travel documents.
Administration of justice and democratic processes: AI systems used in researching and interpreting facts and applying the law to concrete facts or used in alternative dispute resolution. Influencing elections and referenda outcomes or voting behaviour, excluding outputs that do not directly interact with people, like tools used to organise, optimise and structure political campaigns.

The Moral Machine: an experiment on Accountability

The Moral Machine is an MIT-led online platform that presents people with stylized “trolley-problem-like” dilemmas framed as autonomous-vehicle crashes (e.g., swerve vs stay; passengers vs pedestrians) and records their choices. Here you find the article published in Nature in Oct 2028 with the Moral Machine Experiment The associated flagship publication, Awad et al., Nature (2018), “The Moral Machine experiment,” reports collecting about 39.61 million decisions from participants across 233 countries/territories (in multiple languages) and analyzes (a) aggregate global preferences, (b) variation by demographics, and (c) cross-country patterns/clusters. Disclaimers/critique: these results are primarily descriptive—they capture what survey participants say they would choose under simplified prompts, not what is ethically justified or legally acceptable. The scenarios also heavily abstract away real-world driving (uncertainty, feasibility, duty of care, and the dominant safety-engineering goal of reducing risk overall), and the participation is self-selected and uneven across populations, which weakens “this is what a country believes” interpretations. Finally, even if the preference data were perfect, turning it into rules raises legitimacy and governance questions (who decides, by what authority, and how conflicts are resolved). A Nature correspondence later made this point sharply, arguing the Moral Machine experiment is not a sound basis for policymaking.

Ethics of Generative AI: Hallucinations, Bias, and Environmental Impact

Along with the benefits, generative AI raises concerns about misuse and errors. There are some limitations where legal frameworks have not caught up with technological developments. To mitigate these risks and ensure the technology benefits society, the OECD works with governments to enable policies that ensure the ethical and responsible use of generative AI. (This section adapted and extended from OECD Policy Observatory for AI).

AI “hallucinations”, or convincing but inaccurate outputs

When large language models, or textual generative AI, create incorrect yet convincing outputs, it is called a hallucination. This is unintentional and can happen if a correct answer is not found in the training data. Beyond perpetuating inaccurate information, this can interfere with the model’s ability to learn new skills and even lead to a loss of skills.

Fake and misleading content

While generative AI brings efficiencies to content creation, it also poses risks that must be considered carefully. One major concern is the potential for generating fake or misleading content. For example, generative AI can be used to create realistic-looking but entirely fabricated images or videos, which can be used to spread disinformation or deceive people. This poses challenges for the detection and verification of digital media.

Intellectual property right infringement

Generative AI raises intellectual property rights issues, particularly concerning:

  • unlicensed content in training data,
  • potential copyright, patent, and trademark infringement of AI creations,
  • and ownership of AI-generated works.
  • Whether commercial entities can legally train ML models on copyrighted material is contested in Europe and the US. Several lawsuits were filed in the US against companies that allegedly trained their models on copyrighted data without authorisation to make and later store copies of the resulting images. These decisions will set legal precedents and impact the generative AI industry, from start-ups to multinational tech companies.

Job and labour market transformations

Generative AI is likely to transform labour markets and jobs, but exactly how is still uncertain and being debated among experts. Generative AI could automate tasks traditionally performed by humans, leading to job displacement in some industries and professions.

While some jobs might be automated or eliminated, generative AI could transform existing jobs. This could lead to humans performing tasks more efficiently and generating new creative possibilities. This transformation would lead to a shift in required skills.

Addressing these risks will require combining technical solutions, policy frameworks, and responsible practices to ensure that generative AI benefits society while minimising potential harm.

Energy consumption and the environment

Generative AI requires tremendous computing power and consumes natural resources, leading to a significant ecological footprint. Poorly controlled use of generative AI in areas like climate modelling and environmental simulations could unintentionally exacerbate ecological challenges and undermine conservation efforts.

Bias, stereotype amplification and privacy concerns

AI can analyse large amounts of data to extract precious information that humans could not see otherwise. But the risk is an amplification of existing biases present in the training data. If the training data contains biases, such as racial or gender stereotypes, the generative AI model may inadvertently produce biased outputs, such as misleading or inappropriate content. This can perpetuate and even amplify societal inequalities and discrimination.

Generative AI also raises privacy concerns. By training on large amounts of data, these models may inadvertently capture and reproduce private or sensitive information. For example, a language model trained on text data may reproduce personal details or confidential information.

Potential future risks and concerns

In the near term, generative AI can exacerbate challenges as synthetic content with varying quality and accuracy proliferates in digital spaces and is then used to train subsequent generative AI models, triggering a vicious cycle. Over the longer term, emergent behaviours such as increased agency, power-seeking, and pursuing hidden sub-goals to achieve a core objective might not align with human values and intent. If manifested, such behaviours could lead to systemic harms and collective disempowerment. Given these risks, overreliance, trust and dependency on AI could cause deep, long-term harm to societies. And a concentration of AI resources in a few multinational tech companies and governments may lead to a global imbalance.
